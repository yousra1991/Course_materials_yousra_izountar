Who is involved?

Four groups of people show up in nearly every project: the client who sponsors it, the design team who builds it, the target audience it is meant for, and the users who actually touch it. Tap each one, then switch to the sports-shop example.

The client pays, the users click. Classical methods mostly listen to the client. User-oriented methods insist on truly considering the users, not just the client.

The target audience is not "everybody". In the sports-shop example it narrows step by step until it reaches the people the interface is really for. Drag the slider to narrow it.

Solving the right problem

A request almost never arrives as a problem. It arrives as a solution someone has already imagined. The lecture's example: "Add a help button on the home page." Walk through it below.

Limited solution. A help button gives temporary assistance, not a real fix.

Better approach. Re-examine user needs and simplify the process itself.

Think systemically. Take a global view: understand the entire user journey, not just isolated interface elements.

That last idea is exactly what we will practise in the journey-map step of Define.

Why classical methods fall short for interfaces

By the end of this module you can describe four classical software-engineering models, say what each does with users, and list the four characteristics a UI-oriented method needs instead.

Software engineering (SE) is a set of methods and tools to produce and maintain high-quality software. Its aim is to design large systems and solve complex problems. It rests on formalised processes, rigour, modular code and scalability, and it stands against the "quick-and-dirty", unstructured approach.

The classical flow is: client need or designer idea, then a functional specification (what the software does, in business terms, with scenarios and use cases), then a technical specification (how to build it, the architecture). Now meet the models built on that flow.

Model explorer

Pick a model, read what it says about users

The balance sheet

What classical methods do well
  • Structured methods
  • Suitable for large projects
Where they fail for interfaces
  • Limited role of users
  • Process focused mainly on software functionalities
  • The UI is considered too late: usage should be anticipated, in parallel with functionalities
  • Late evaluation
  • Methods not well suited to UI design

Why this matters: the interface is half the bill

The user interface is a key point of an application. It is also a source of errors and extra costs: the lecture puts the UI at about 50% of development cost and about 50% of change requests. Tap the cards to flip them.

What a UI-oriented method needs

In short: a UI-oriented method is user-centred, prototype-based, evaluated early and progressive.

The Double Diamond

By the end of this module you can explain diverging and converging, name the four phases and their guiding questions, and say how the model answers the limits you just met.

The Double Diamond draws a design process as two diamonds. In each one, you first widen (diverge: explore many possibilities) and then narrow (converge: decide). The first diamond finds the right problem. The second builds the right solution.

Where it comes from. The lecture presents it through Don Norman, who describes it in The Design of Everyday Things. The diagram itself was published by the UK Design Council in 2005 with the phases Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver, and it adapts a divergence-convergence idea from Béla Bánáthy (1996). The lecture's own labels (immersion, definition, ideation, prototyping, test) describe the same movements.

Discover: explore the situation

By the end of this module you can choose and plan information-gathering methods, run a short brainstorm, write critical-incident questions, and recognise what a good Discover harvest looks like.

Discover is the diverging half of the first diamond. The guiding question is "what is the nature of the problem?" You are not looking for the answer yet. You are gathering information about the market, the tasks and the users, and the same tools serve both design and evaluation.

Running example. Management of a sports shop asked for "a help button". Your job in Discover is to find out what is really going on for shoppers, sales staff and administrators. All findings here are fictional training data.

The toolbox

Tap a method to read its objective, procedure and watch-outs. Filter by who takes part.

Three more tools from the lecture's list live elsewhere in the process: card sorting appears in module 5 (information architecture); parallel design and the Wizard of Oz method belong to the second diamond (module 6).

Try it: brainstorming

Brainstorming generates a large number of creative ideas for a specific design problem. It has two phases: generate without judging, then sort and select. Run a mini session on the real problem.

Try it: plan an observation

Observation collects data on how users really perform tasks. The lecture asks for at least two independent users, a defined task, and a decision on what to measure.

Try it: ask about real incidents

A critical-incident interview takes place in the user's real work environment. You ask them to recall a specific problem from recently, describe each incident in detail, and say what was usual or unusual. The technique goes back to Flanagan's work in the 1950s. Sort these questions.

Define: turn findings into a challenge

By the end of this module you can build a persona, prioritise problems on an impact-effort matrix, map a user journey, and organise content with card sorting, then write one sentence that names the real problem.

Define is the converging half of the first diamond. The guiding question is "which problem is the most relevant?" and the output is the challenge identified, the real problem. Four tools get you there.

PersonaWho are we solving this for? Priority matrixWhich problems matter most? Journey mapHow does the problem unfold over time? Information architectureHow must content be organised?

The order is a sensible default, not a law. Real teams loop between these tools as they learn.

Persona: who are we solving this for?

A persona represents future users through a realistic fictional profile. Each one reflects a segment of the target audience, different roles call for different personas (administrator, end user), and each includes socio-demographic and psychological traits. Personas are used in all phases of design and evaluation. The idea was popularised by Alan Cooper.

Advantages
  • Helps designers adopt the user's perspective and build empathy
  • Acts as a shared communication tool within the team
  • Online tools exist: Xtensio, MakeMyPersona
Limitations
  • Difficult to define truly relevant personas
  • Risk of stereotyping users

Persona workshop

Load a preset or edit the fields

Design for all, not for yourself. Relying only on your own profile excludes others, through gender, origin or skin colour, age or social class, language or technological skill, and physical or cognitive disability. The lecture's warning example is a soap dispenser that failed to detect dark skin tones, which shows why you test with diverse users. Ground every trait in Discover data so a persona does not become a stereotype.

From persona to scenario

A use case takes the user's perspective, in the user's language, and is goal-oriented. It represents a user objective and one way of using the application. Combine a persona with a use case and you get a scenario you can later test.

Persona (who)+Use case (what for)=Scenario (the story)→Evaluation (success, errors, steps, time, satisfaction)

Priority matrix: which problems matter most?

Discover leaves you with more problems and ideas than you can handle. A priority matrix forces explicit trade-offs: each item is placed by its impact (value for users and the business) and its effort (cost, time, risk). That gives four zones: quick wins (high impact, low effort), major projects, fill-ins and reconsider. This impact-effort grid is a common design-team practice; it descends from the urgent-versus-important idea popularised by Covey.

Impact should come from Discover evidence and your persona, not from the loudest voice. Drag the numbered markers (or focus one and use the arrow keys). Item 12 is the original request.

Other lenses. MoSCoW sorts requirements into Must, Should, Could and Won't (this time). Use it when you need to negotiate scope with a client; use impact-effort when you need to choose where to start.

Journey map: how does the problem unfold over time?

A journey map tells the story of one persona achieving one goal, phase by phase: what they do, think and feel, where they struggle, and where design could help. It is how you put the lecture's "think systemically" into practice: see the whole path, not a single screen. The process usually starts from a skeleton of goals and actions, adds thoughts and emotions, and ends in a visual that teams can share.

Information architecture: how must content be organised?

Information architecture (IA) is the structural design of an information space so people can find what they need. Its working parts are organisation (how things are grouped), labelling (what they are called), navigation (how people move) and search. The lecture's tool for the first of these is card sorting, used to organise application features and to define or improve the information architecture.

Method
  • Gather users representative of the target audience
  • Each card is a feature or piece of information
  • Users group the cards that belong together, then name the groups (menus or categories)
Two variants
  • Open sorting: free grouping
  • Closed sorting: groups predefined by designers
  • Run it with several people and compare the groupings

Inclusive design in every phase

Inclusive design considers all potential users, not only the designer, so that nothing is omitted and nobody is discriminated against. It applies whatever the make-up of the design team, and at every stage from design to evaluation. Here is what that looks like in the phases you just practised (the questions are my suggestions).

Choosing a design method

An effective method is iterative and progressive, evaluates early, relies on prototypes, and actively involves users. You choose or adapt it by project size and complexity, level of innovation, and available time, money and people, always considering inclusion and digital sobriety.
