Introduction

Why evaluate?

Evaluation is a key phase of the design process, inside an iterative loop. We use it to test the solutions we produced: validate them and propose improvements.

What do we evaluate?

Usability (ISO 9241)

Usability = the degree to which a product can be used by identified users, to achieve defined goals, in a specified context of use, with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction.

Same task, three results. Pick a scenario: which of the three measures is poor?

Learnability = how quickly one gets to grips with the software. Intuitiveness = usable the very first time, without training. UX is broader: it includes all the aspects of the experience.

Evaluation approaches

Two big distinctions organise every evaluation technique.

Analytical vs experimentalBefore or after developmentQuantitative vs qualitativeNumbers or explanations

Analytical or experimental?

Analytical approach
  • A priori evaluation: before development or deployment
  • No system and no users required
  • Based on theoretical models of human functioning
  • Uses standards and criteria

Predictive models (GOMS), cognitive inspection, ergonomic audit.

Experimental approach
  • Rather a posteriori: after development or deployment
  • A system and users are required
  • Collect and analyse users' behaviour data
  • Objective data (recordings) and subjective data (asking users)

User tests: in real situation or in the lab.

Sort it. Is each situation analytical or experimental?
Analytical techniques in a nutshell

GOMS (Goal, Operator, Method, Selection rule) describes hierarchically how the system is used and predicts error-free performance. Cognitive inspection (cognitive walk-through): experts change hats and imagine what the persona would do with a task and its action sequence. Do they understand the messages and the behaviour of the system?

Quantitative or qualitative?

Quantitative

Goal: show facts by quantifying a phenomenon.

+ objective measures, numbers and statistics, automatic processing.

− does not explain why.

Qualitative

Goal: help understand or explain a phenomenon.

+ interpretation, descriptive and textual results.

− manual, longer processing.

Which is it? Classify each result.

Perception and 5-second tests

Show a static, graphically finished mock-up and ask what the user understands.

Perception testQuestions to ask5-second testTry it yourself

Perception test (qualitative)

Principle

Show a mock-up to a user (the tester), analyse how they understand it, and ask about their perception.

What: a static, graphically finished mock-up, or a screenshot of an existing app.
When: at every stage of design.

+ / −

+ fast, early, little equipment, easy to analyse.

− no in-depth analysis: complete with richer methods.

Questions to ask

  • What can you do with this screen?
  • What do you see first?
  • Which actions are possible? (identify the action zones and buttons, their meaning, their use)
  • To order a pizza for tonight, what would you do? What will you find next?
Practice. Here is a screen. Write down your answers, then compare with what the designer intended.

5-second test

A variant of the perception test to measure the first impression (features and look). Show the screen for 5 seconds, then collect impressions in a short questionnaire. Very fast, but unsuitable for complex screens, and the impression may come from things you cannot change (the logo).

Eye tracking

Find out what the user looks at, with cameras or a webcam.

Principle and limitsFixations and saccadesHeat map labMove the pointer, build a map

Eye tracking (qualitative)

Principle

Calibration, then tracking and recording of:

  • gaze direction (fixations) → heat map
  • eye movements (saccades) → scan path map

Tools: physical cameras, a webcam online (e.g. GazeRecorder), online eye-tracker simulators (e.g. Feng-GUI). How many: at least 40 users.

+ / −

+ precise, concrete results, comparisons.

− costly, heavy, constraining; reliability problems; better for interfaces than for interactions; gives findings but no explanation.

Fixations or saccades?

Heat map lab

A heat map turns the intensity of a variable (time of gaze, clicks, pointer positions) into a colour in two dimensions: more colour = more intensity. Move your pointer over the page: you are the eye tracker. Tap to simulate clicks.

Tests with observation

A user performs predefined tasks; their interactions are observed and analysed.

MethodWho, where, how manyHow to run onePrepare, run, analyseHow many users?Nielsen's curveReading resultsDashboards, heat maps, paths

Tests with observation (qualitative)

Principle

A user does a set of predefined tasks. Their interactions with the system are observed, then analysed.

What: interactive mock-up, prototype or working system.
Who: testers or end users.
Where: in natural context or in a controlled situation (usability lab); remote, synchronous with an evaluator or asynchronous with an automatic tool.
When: as early as possible during design.

+ / −

+ concrete, rich, leads to recommendations; flexible; combinable with questionnaires and interviews.

− longer to organise and analyse; artificial scenarios; only some functions are tested.

How many users?

5 users detect about 80% of the problems (Jakob Nielsen). 20 users give statistically valid results.

Nielsen's model: the share of problems found is 1 − (1 − L)n, where L is the share of problems one user finds (about 31% in Nielsen's studies). Change L and n.

Analysis and results

  • Closed questions / figures: sum, average.
  • Open questions / observations: synthesise, identify themes, count occurrences (thematic content analysis).
  • Video or audio: very long and costly to analyse, but removes doubts.
  • Computer traces: all actions are stored, so the session can be replayed. Plan them before the test.

Then interpret (find causes) and exploit (correct and improve the system).

Interviews and A/B tests

Two very different tools: one explains, the other decides.

InterviewsOpen questions, 45 to 90 minA/B test simulatorWhich version wins?

Interviews (qualitative)

Principle

The user answers the questions of an interviewer, to complete other tests with qualitative information that can explain quantitative results.

How many: about ten users. Duration: 45 to 90 minutes. Who: representative users + members of the design team. Where: quiet place (or video call). When: just after the other tests.

+ / −

+ open questions, rich information, helps understand the user's point of view and processes.

− long to run and analyse; hard to generalise.

Rewrite the question. Pick the better interview question.

A/B test (quantitative)

Two versions identical except for one detail run in production in parallel. Part of the users see A, the others B. Compare their efficiency on predefined criteria. A multivariate test combines variations (2 colours × 2 positions = 4 versions).

How to run one
  1. Objectives: concerned screen, variants, performance indicators (time spent, order rate...).
  2. Hypotheses: e.g. variant B makes people order more, or faster.
  3. Split: classic 50/50, or less risky 90% current / 10% tested.
  4. Run: deploy both versions + random assignment.
  5. Analyse: is A really more efficient than B, significantly? Then define a new hypothesis for a new test.

Recommendation: about 10,000 users per variant. Limits: no explanation (why A>B?), only details, needs many users.

In practice

No technique is enough alone. Choose according to your project and your objective, then combine.

Choose the techniqueProject, time, objectiveThe evaluation processFrom objective to conclusionsBeware of biasWhat spoils the results

Technique chooser

Criteria: scale of the project, time, technical, financial and human means, number of available users, and do not forget inclusion.

The evaluation process

Example: checking that OPALE (a campus app) suits first-year students. Click each step.

Combine methods. Quantitative methods are fast and efficient (a questionnaire to 1,000 students). Qualitative methods are heavy but rich (interviews with 5 students from different backgrounds). Repeat tests at different moments, on more or less advanced versions: that is continuous improvement.

Bias

Problems: the test conditions distort the results, or the results look good while the software still has weaknesses. Spot the biased plan.
