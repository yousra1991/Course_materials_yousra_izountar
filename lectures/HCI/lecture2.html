How to measure ergonomics?

Usability comes first, then the audit method, then the theories the audit relies on.

Usability / ergonomics (reminder)

Effectivenessachieving the expected result
Efficiencyminimal resource consumption (effort, time)
Satisfactioncomfort and subjective evaluation
Contextidentified users, defined goals, specified context of use

The degree to which a product can be used by 👤 identified users, to achieve 🎯 defined goals, in a 💻 specified context of use, with ✅ effectiveness, ⚡ efficiency and 😊 satisfaction.

Evaluation method

  • The ergonomic audit.

Example: booking a train ticket

Ergonomic audit: where, what, how…

Objectiveevaluate weaknesses and strengths of the interface from an ergonomic point of view; identify problems users may encounter.
Whatmockup, prototype or functional application.
Whoan ergonomics expert (not end users).
Wherein a "laboratory" setting.
Whenredesign, as early as possible; creation, as soon as a mockup/app is available.

Audit Principles ±

🎯 Principle
  • Identify context, constraints, user characteristics.
  • Analyse the interface following user pathways.
  • Identify problems.
  • Prioritise fixes by importance and impact.
👥 How Many Experts?
  • Several, to reduce subjectivity: 3 to 5 (1–2 in practice).
  • 1 ergonomist + 1 domain expert (business knowledge).
✅ Advantages
  • Detailed analysis based on scientific knowledge + expert experience.
⚠️ Limitations
  • Impossible to respect all ergonomic criteria → compromises needed.
  • Does not replace usability testing.

📌 To remember: The audit is done by experts on a mockup or app; usability tests, with real users, stay necessary.

Why scientific theories?

How do we evaluate the ergonomics of an application?

  • Opinions of designers, colleagues or users ("ugly", "hurts my eyes") → not constructive.
  • Usability tests → useful, but cannot reveal all problems.
  • Ergonomic audits → but on what do experts rely?

Cognitive theories

How users remember, decide and wait.

Miller's Magic Number7 ± 2 elements in working memory. Hick's LawThe time to decide depends on the number and complexity of options. 2-Second RuleThe response should appear in under 2 seconds. Principle of 3 ClicksThe information must be within 3 clicks (web).

Miller's Magic Number Cognitive

Working memory can contain 7 elements ± 2, depending on the individual, fatigue…

Risks
  • The user forgets and wastes time searching.
  • Particularly for software used infrequently.
Solutions
  • Limit the number of items to 7.
  • Avoid unnecessary information.
  • Establish links between elements: via color, format, location…

Hick's Law Cognitive

The time to decide depends on the number and complexity of options.

Risks
  • The user may make mistakes or lose time.
Solutions
  • Limit the number of choices.
  • Remove useless information.
  • Use concise, clear messages.

Enrichment: Hick–Hyman formula T = b·log₂(n+1). The image also warns: do not over-simplify.

Interactive Hick's Law Comparison

Toggle between the bad practice (overwhelming choices) and the optimized implementation based on Hick's Law.

2-Second Rule Cognitive

The system's response should appear in under 2 seconds.

Risks
  • The user may repeat the action.
  • Bugs or error messages may appear.
Solutions
  • Waiting cursor (short actions).
  • Feedback indicator (short actions).
  • Progress indicator (long actions).

Principle of 3 Clicks Cognitive

The desired information must be accessible within 3 clicks (web) and quickly.

Risks
  • The user may abandon the task/application.
Solutions
  • Make important actions accessible quickly.
  • Adapt to the context.

Perceptual theories

How the eye and brain organise what is on the screen.

Gestalt theory: perceptionCompletion: we see a whole form. Law of ProximityThe brain groups what is physically close. Law of SimilarityThe brain associates what looks alike. Reading on screen: scan patternsZ-pattern, F-pattern. Reading on screen: "above the fold"Main content visible without scrolling.

Gestalt theory: perception Perceptual

Principle
  • Perception treats phenomena as structured sets (forms).
  • Not as a simple addition of elements.
Completion
  • Emergence of missing characteristics.
Gestalt laws
  • Proximity, similarity.
  • Figure-ground, continuity, symmetry, focal point.

Law of Proximity Perceptual

The brain groups what is physically close.

Risks
  • Associating opposite concepts or actions.
Solutions
  • Group similar elements; separate different ones.
  • Use empty space at micro and macro levels.

Law of Similarity Perceptual

The brain tends to associate what looks alike (shape, colour, size, behaviour…).

Risks
  • Confusing similar-looking objects.
Solutions
  • Associate common features with similar elements.
  • Mark different elements with specific features (including links).

Reading on screen: scan patterns Perceptual

Classic reading direction
  • From left to right; from past to future.
Screen scan patterns
  • First visual exploration of the screen: Z-pattern scanning.
  • Then selective scanning.
  • Search engine behaviour: F-pattern or comb-shaped pattern.

Place the logo and login at the top, the main call to action where the Z ends (bottom right).

Reading on screen: "above the fold" Perceptual

  • The main information of a document must be visible above the fold. Example: the title of a newspaper even when folded / the content of a folded leaflet.
  • The main content of a web page must be visible without scrolling.

Motor interaction theories

How users recognise what is actionable and reach it.

Affordance (potentiality)Possibility of action suggested by object characteristics. Fitts' LawTime to reach a target: its size and its distance.

Affordance (potentiality) Motor

Principle
  • Possibility of action suggested by object characteristics.
  • Implicit or explicit signs.
Risks
  • Hesitation, time loss.
  • Wrong or no usage.
Solutions
  • Help distinguish clickable / non-clickable elements.
  • Encourage interaction through form, label, color, placement.

Fitts' Law Motor

Principle
  • The time (T) needed to reach a target (a clickable element) depends on its size (width) and the distance (D) from the pointer.
  • Better accessibility: large component + in the center of the screen.
  • But some functionalities must be "protected".
Risks
  • Loss of time; clicks next to the target.
Solutions
  • Respect the organization of the screen.
  • Adapt sizes and locations.
  • Propose contextual menus.

Formula: T = a + b·log₂(2D/W) (the slide calls the width L; the figure calls it W).

Each colour topic has live examples: contrast checker, number of colours, text on pictures, usual codes, culture, a student's week, colour-blind simulator.

Text display Visual design

Principles of typography
  • Sans-serif fonts are more readable on screen: Arial, Calibri, Helvetica, Geneva, Aptos…
  • Styles slow down reading and are less readable: bold, italics, underlined (+ confusion with links), CAPITALS (+ feeling of shouting).
  • Ban COMBINATIONS (bold + underlined).
Risks
  • Loss of readability; slowing down reading.
Solutions
  • Use styles sparingly, to highlight certain elements.

Readability of colors Visual design

Principle
  • The eye needs a high contrast between information and background.
  • Too many colors disturb the gaze.
Risks
  • Reduced legibility; difficulty identifying text and icons.
Solutions
  • Dark characters on a light background, preferably black on white; except in low-light environments (night, specific tasks).
  • Avoid some colour combinations and colors that are too vivid.
  • Limit the number of colors (7 maximum, excluding icons).
  • Avoid colour gradients; be careful with text over an image.

Meaning of colors Visual design

Principle
  • Colors have a usual meaning, a symbolism.
  • Example: red = stop / green = go.
  • Counter-example: in China, red = joy; on stage, green = superstition.
Risks
  • Misunderstanding of the interface or the colour code.
Solutions
  • Follow usual codes: green = validation, success; red = warning, stop, failure; neutral colors = nothing special.
  • Use neutral colors when there is no specific need (default = black).
  • Pay attention to local and cultural specificities.

Meaning of colors: examples Examples

First follow the usual codes, then check that they also hold for your users.

Relevance of colors Visual design

Use colors to convey something in a relevant way; ensure a good match between colour and concept.

Risks
  • Objects with the same colour are wrongly associated.
  • Similar concepts are not associated.
Solutions
  • Same type of information ⇒ same colour.
  • Different types ⇒ contrasting colors.
  • Similar types ⇒ slightly contrasting colors.
  • Combine / make information explicit.

Portability of colors Visual design

Information carried by colour must remain available everywhere, always, for everyone.

Risks
  • Poor rendering (distinction, contrast, number of colors) outdoors or at night; on projectors or smartphones; for colour-blind users (about 10% of men, 0.5% of women).
Solutions: interface testing tools
  • Simulators: Colblindor (online), Let's Get Color Blind (Chrome), Colorsimulations (Windows), Chromatic Vision Simulator (mobile).
  • Contrast tests: Contrast Checker, etc.

Self-assessment

Answer in your head, then click to check.

What are the three components of usability?

Effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction.

Who performs an ergonomic audit, and does it replace usability testing?

An ergonomics expert (ideally with a domain expert), and no: it does not replace usability testing.

How many items can working memory hold?

7 ± 2 (Miller).

What does Hick's law say?

Decision time depends on the number and complexity of options.

Which indicator suits a long action?

A progress indicator (waiting cursor/feedback for short ones; response under 2 seconds).

Which Gestalt law justifies grouping a menu by category?

Proximity; similarity for shared appearance.

Which scan pattern is typical of first exploration, and which of search engines?

Z-pattern for first exploration; F-pattern for search engines.

What does Fitts' law imply for a button?

Larger and closer targets are reached faster.

Name three colour guidelines for interfaces.

High contrast (dark on light); at most 7 colors; follow usual codes and test for colour blindness.
