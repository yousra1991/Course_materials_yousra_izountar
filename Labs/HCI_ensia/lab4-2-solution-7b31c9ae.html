From Users to Interfaces - part II:
Building the Blueprint - Low & Med-Fidelity Prototyping

There is no single right answer. The students design their own prototype, so this guide says what a good answer contains, what to accept, and what to refuse. Mark the reasoning, not the drawing skill: a clean sketch with no link to the personas is worth less than a rough sketch that answers a real need.

What the lab is really testing

  1. Can they choose a fidelity, and say why? (Lecture 6: stage, goals, resources.)
  2. Does the prototype come from the personas and scenarios of Lab 4 (I), or was it invented from nothing?
  3. Can they move the same concept from desktop to mobile without just shrinking it?
  4. Can they test as a persona and give feedback about a need, not about taste?

Activity 1 — From Ideas to Wireframes

A. Fidelity and tools

Accept: low fidelity on paper for the first ideas, then a mid-fidelity clickable wireframe in Balsamiq. The justification should name the stage ("the flow is not fixed yet"), the goal ("we want to know if they find the comparison") and the resources ("one session").
Refuse, or ask again: "we chose high fidelity because it is more beautiful". Colours and real photos at this stage attract comments on the look and hide the problems of the flow. This is the mistake the Amar Darek case was made for.

B. The screens

A workable set for the Car Showroom case is five to seven screens. Typically:

ScreenWhat it must contain
HomeSearch field, two or three shortcuts (new, used, by budget), one clear entry per persona.
Browse / resultsFilters (price, year, fuel, power), sorting, the list of cars with the information that matters, not just a photo.
Car detailPhotos, specifications, price, availability, the main action (book a test drive, contact the seller).
CompareTwo or three cars side by side, the same rows for each.
Test drive / contactShort form: date, place, phone. Confirmation screen after it.
Saved / favouritesWhat the student kept, so they can come back.
A good sheet says, for each screen, what the user can do and where it leads. That is what makes it a prototype and not a drawing.

C. Desktop to mobile

Expect real adaptation decisions, not "the same but smaller":

ElementWebsiteMobile, and the reason
NavigationHorizontal menu, everything visibleBottom bar with 3 to 5 items, or a burger menu; the thumb reaches the bottom of the screen.
FiltersSide panel, always openA "Filter" button that opens a sheet; the screen is too narrow to keep them visible.
ComparisonThree cars side by sideTwo cars at most, or swipe between them; three columns are unreadable at 375 px.
Main actionButton in the pageButton fixed at the bottom, full width, near the thumb (Lecture 4, screen 2 of CampusBus).
ImagesLarge galleryOne image plus swipe; the data pack matters.

D. Information structure

Accept: 4 to 7 top-level items, at most 3 levels, names in the users' words ("Find a car", "Compare", "Book a test drive"), not in the showroom's words ("Stock management", "CRM").
Refuse: a flat menu of 12 items, or names taken from the database tables.

E. Personas

Each persona must reach their goal, and the number of taps should be reasonable (three is the usual target).

PersonaGoalWhat the prototype must show
Road Trip EnthusiastA reliable car with range and boot spaceFilters on consumption, boot volume, range; comparison of two cars on those rows.
Weekend Racer (Tifosi)Power, feeling, the brandPerformance figures in front, photos and video, test-drive booking in one or two taps.
Student on a BudgetThe cheapest car that still worksPrice filter first, total cost (insurance, consumption), used cars, monthly payment.
Negative persona(not a user we design for)The sheet should say what they deliberately did not build, and why. This is the part most groups forget.

Activity 3 — Peer testing and feedback loop

A good finding has four parts: what happened (a fact), why it is a problem for that persona, how serious it is, and a precise suggestion. Example: "Playing the Student on a Budget, I could not find the price filter on the mobile home screen; I went back twice and gave up. High. Put a 'Budget' shortcut on the home screen."
Refuse: "it is nice", "I like the colours", "add more photos". These are tastes, not findings, and the lab asks for feedback about user needs.

Check that part D is filled: a group that received four remarks and changed nothing has not done the loop. They may keep a remark, but then they must say why.

Activity 4 — Reflection circle: expected answers

A. How did the personas influence your design decisions?

Expected: the personas decided what comes first on the screen and what was left out. Concrete examples are required: the budget filter on the home page because of the student, the performance figures in front because of the Tifosi. An answer with no example is incomplete.

B. Desktop and mobile

Expected: space and attention. On the website everything can stay visible; on mobile you choose, you hide behind buttons, you put the main action near the thumb, you reduce the number of items per screen, and you keep the network and the screen size in mind. A good answer says that the content priority changes, not only the size.

C. What was easier, what was harder

Expected easier: drawing fast, changing an idea, throwing a screen away without regret, discussing with the group around a sheet of paper. Expected harder: deciding what to leave out, keeping the whole flow coherent, making the wireframe clickable, and accepting that it looks unfinished when you show it. Accept the honest answer "people commented on the look instead of the flow", which is exactly the lesson of Lecture 6.

Marking guide (on 20)

CriterionPoints
Website and mobile prototypes exist, both of them, at a stated fidelity that is justified4
The screens are listed with what the user can do and where it leads (a real flow, not loose drawings)3
Desktop to mobile: at least three real adaptation decisions, each with a reason3
Information structure: 4 to 7 items, 3 levels at most, users' words2
Every persona reaches their goal; the negative persona is handled3
Peer testing: findings with fact, reason, severity and suggestion3
Feedback loop: the remarks received are turned into named changes1
Reflection: answered with examples from their own work1

Common mistakes to watch for

  • A beautiful high-fidelity screen made in one hour, with no flow behind it.
  • The mobile version is the website shrunk, with no decision taken.
  • Personas from Lab 4 (I) never mentioned again: the prototype could be anybody's.
  • Feedback about taste rather than about a user need.
  • Remarks received and never used: no loop, so the next lab starts from the wrong prototype.
  • The negative persona forgotten: nothing says what the team refused to build.
