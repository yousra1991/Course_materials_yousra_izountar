Unit III · Lecture 6 · Semester S7Course summary

Prototyping: Low, Mid and High Fidelity

Learning goals. Tell low, mid and high fidelity apart and choose the right level for a given stage, question and cost. Build a simple prototype from a persona and a scenario, and use it to test a design idea with users.

0. Opening case: which one do you show first?

Amar Darek — a mobile app for buying home furniture

Your team wants to test the first design with five customers, and you have one afternoon to prepare the prototype and run the test. Three versions exist: a paper sketch (low), a clickable wireframe (mid) and a finished-looking screen (high). Which one do you show?

The question is not which prototype is best. It is what you need to learn from the user, in the cheapest way.

Answer for this case. The question to answer is: can customers add a sofa to their cart without help? That is about flow and navigation, so a clickable wireframe (mid fidelity) fits. Use the paper sketch (low) if the flow itself is still unclear.

Why not high fidelity?

  • Time: you cannot build it in one afternoon.
  • Wrong feedback: people comment on colours and fonts instead of the flow.
  • Avoidance of criticism: users hesitate to criticise something that looks finished.
  • Cost of change: it is expensive to drop or redo.

Link with Lecture 5: the scenario joins a persona (the customer), a goal (buy furniture) and a context (using Amar Darek).

1. What a prototype is

  • An early sample, model or release of a system, built to test and evaluate a concept or an interaction.
  • A tool that bridges the gap between a design on paper and a real, working product.
  • It lets you explore ideas and show the intention behind a feature before spending time and money on development.
  • It can give the look and feel of the product without necessarily having the working parts.

2. Why prototyping matters

Finds usability problems earlyUser testing shows the problems before the final development.
Communicates the designA tangible idea for clients, developers and the rest of the team.
Tests design choicesSeveral approaches are compared and refined.
Gathers user feedbackAn iterative loop that keeps the product user-centred.
Saves time laterThe prototyping phase costs a little now and avoids delays later.
Reduces costSolving a problem early avoids expensive rework.

3. Prototyping, HCD and UCD

Prototyping is the step where human-centred design becomes visible. In HCD and UCD the user is involved all along, and the prototype is the object that carries the question to the user: it is built, shown, evaluated, then changed. Without a prototype there is nothing concrete to evaluate, and the design stays an opinion.

4. Creation and co-creation

Prototype creation — designing based on users' needs

  1. Creation sessions: generate ideas and concepts for the design.
  2. Design workshops: the team works together on the prototype.
  3. Card sorting / affinity diagramming: organise the information.
  4. Personas and journey mapping: find the pain points and the opportunities.
  5. Task mapping: design the prototype around the users' tasks.

Prototype co-creation — designing with users

  1. Co-creation sessions: users generate ideas with the team.
  2. Participatory design workshops: users design with their hands.
  3. Card sorting and affinity diagramming done by the users themselves.
  4. Journey mapping: users describe their own steps and pain points.

Example: a university mobile application

Creation: the team studies the students' needs, identifies the main tasks (checking grades, viewing schedules, accessing courses) and builds a prototype.
Co-creation: students take part in workshops, suggest features, organise the information and help design the navigation and the workflows.

5. The three types of prototype

FidelityPurposeCharacteristicsExamples
Low Early visualisation, exploring the concept, brainstorming. Simple, quick, cheap, easy to change. Hand-drawn sketches (layouts, elements, user flows) · paper prototypes (cut-outs of screens the user can move) · storyboards, like a comic strip of the interaction over time.
Mid Refining the design, testing the basic interactions, early feedback. More detailed and interactive than low, but still abstract: no real colours, no real content. Wireframes, the digital skeleton showing structure and layout · interactive mock-ups in Figma or Adobe XD that simulate the main flows.
High Simulating the final product, testing complex interactions, detailed feedback. Highly interactive, visually finished, realistic content and animations. Functional prototypes partly coded · coded prototypes in HTML, CSS and JavaScript · interactive design prototypes in Figma with real navigation and transitions.

6. Choosing the right fidelity

Project stageEarly stages benefit from low fidelity; later stages need higher fidelity.
GoalsExploring concepts → low fidelity. Testing a precise interaction → higher fidelity.
ResourcesTime, budget and skills decide what is actually feasible.

Effort grows with fidelity: sketches → wireframes (can be clickable) → mock-ups → software prototypes. The curve is steep at the end, so the last level costs much more than the first.

7. Stakeholders

A stakeholder is any group or individual who can affect, or is affected by, the achievement of the organisation's objectives (Freeman, 1984). In app design: anyone who can influence the app or is affected by it.

8. AI and prototyping

From the case study on rapid UI prototyping with "vibe coding" (Li, Maheshwari and Voelker, 2025).

ResultsAI layouts gave diverse alternatives and one new idea (users build filters by choosing fields, operators and values). Interactive prototypes improved the feedback: users held back on sketches and low-fidelity designs, but engaged and found real issues with interactive ones.
WeaknessesRepeated prompting in one session produced cumulative errors, and code generated from screenshots reproduced the visuals but not the interactions.
ConclusionsGenerative UI speeds up communication and feedback, but brings code errors, security concerns, and a risk of converging on conventional patterns. When to bring AI in depends on the team's experience; prompt writing and domain knowledge are both needed.
LimitsOne case study, one team, no quantitative results.

Key takeaways

  1. Design → Prototype → Evaluate → Refine. The goal is not a perfect prototype at once, but a design that improves step by step with user feedback.
  2. Whether the prototype is made by hand or with AI tools, the objective is the same: design solutions that meet users' needs. AI can speed up the work; it cannot replace understanding users and evaluating usability.
